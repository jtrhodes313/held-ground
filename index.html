Held Ground

Held Ground is a cultural-cartographic investigation into grief, displacement, and botanical memory. It works from archaeological pollen and macrobotanical evidence recovered from New York's African burial grounds: jimsonweed seed laid on a coffin lid, cattail fiber, honewort and goosefoot-type pollen left as offering or medicine. From that evidence, the project maps what was planted, what was carried, and what was substituted when the original plant could not survive the crossing.

I am exploring cartography as excavation. Underneath the burial coordinates, drawn from primary excavation records, sit the native plant ranges, showing where these species actually grew, and beneath that, the West African relatives of the species found in the graves and the documented ritual uses of those plants on both sides of the Atlantic. Read together, the layers trace how a people's relationship to land, medicine, and mourning can persist even when the land itself is taken twice, first by enslavement, then by erasure.

As spiritual practice and botanical knowledge traveled through the slave trade, they were remade under duress. Plants capable of altering consciousness or inducing profound stupor, jimsonweed among them, came to be associated with transitionary rites and, at times, poison. Enslaved practitioners, known as rootworkers or conjure doctors, drew on local North American plant knowledge and merged it with African spiritualities to build something new out of necessity. Jimsonweed's alkaloids found their way into poultices and specific remedies. Its flowers were laid on coffins as burial offerings. Broad-leaf cattail, likely ingested with water before death, may have served a similar transitionary purpose.

The title comes from Dagara elder Sobonfu Somé's writing on communal grief, where grief isn't a private affliction to manage alone but something a community holds together, witnessed and shared. Held Ground takes that as its premise: what enslaved practitioners built on this ground was ritual grown from necessity, memory, and the plants at hand, and it still holds.
